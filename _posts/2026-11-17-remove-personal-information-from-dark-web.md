A website can take down a copy it hosts. A leaked file can also be downloaded and reposted by anyone who already has it. After a dark-web alert, focus on two things you can verify. Report any specific copy you can identify through an official channel, and secure the accounts or records that the exposed information could be used to access.

## Read the alert before choosing what to remove

Check which company or service the alert names, what data was exposed and when the breach happened. An alert you received today can concern a breach from years ago. If the alert only names your email address, check the original breach notice or your monitoring provider’s detail view to see whether it also identifies a password, payment data or an identity number. Open the affected company’s site directly to check an unexpected notice. [FTC advice after a data breach](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2022/10/have-you-been-affected-data-breach-read)

Suppose a watch service flags your email alongside a password from an old forum. Changing the password for that forum matters if the account still exists. Check where else you used the same password, especially email and financial accounts. A deleted forum profile leaves those other passwords active until you change them.

## Take down any copy you can identify

- **A public post or file with a known URL.** Use the platform’s official privacy or abuse report and provide the URL and the field that needs removal. If it also appears in Google, you can request removal of eligible private information from Search. Check the source and search result separately. [Google’s private-information removal policy](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/9673730?hl=en)
- **A people-search listing that shows the same details.** Use that broker’s opt-out process and check the live listing again after it responds. A new confirmation tells you which request reached the broker. A follow-up visit tells you what the public page now shows. [Data broker opt-out template]({% post_url 2026-11-17-data-broker-opt-out-request-template %})
- **A breach notice with no identifiable public listing.** Ask the affected company what information was exposed and what recovery or account protections it offers. The answer helps you decide which credentials to change or records to monitor. [IdentityTheft.gov data breach guidance](https://www.identitytheft.gov/databreach)

## Act on the particular data that leaked

| Exposed data | What you can do now |
| --- | --- |
| Password | Set a new, unique password at the affected service and anywhere you reused the old one. Enable multi-factor authentication where available. |
| Bank details or payment card | Contact your bank or card issuer through its official number and ask how to secure the account and handle unfamiliar transactions. |
| U.S. Social Security number | Consider a free credit freeze with each of the three nationwide credit bureaus. A freeze limits access to your credit report for new credit applications. |
| Home address or phone number | Remove reachable copies from listings and published files. Verify the updated page and any search results separately. |

If you see signs of identity theft, the FTC’s IdentityTheft.gov tool can help you build a recovery plan in the United States. Keep a private record of when you changed a password, froze credit or verified that a listing disappeared. That gives you a way to act on the next alert even if it concerns the same breach. [Data breach recovery steps](https://www.identitytheft.gov/databreach) · [FTC credit freeze guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/09/get-credit-freeze-stop-identity-thieves)
