Think about everything that passes through your internet provider. Every bill you paid. Every address you lived at. The credit check they ran when you signed up. Your call records. Where your phone was when it connected to a tower. Which customer service rep you yelled at in 2019, and the recording of it.

Now think about the fact that you cancelled that account three moves ago, and most of it is probably still sitting on a server somewhere.

People spend a lot of energy deleting Facebook and scrubbing Google results ([I wrote about that yesterday](/posts/2026/11/21/how-to-delete-your-data-social-media-dating-apps-google/)). Almost nobody thinks about the ISP. That is a mistake, because your phone and internet company holds some of the most complete records about your daily life that exist, and the law actually gives you real tools to make them clean it up.

This guide walks through how to do it, in Canada and the US, with the actual wording of the rules so you can quote them back to the company.

## What your ISP actually has

It helps to know what you are asking for. A typical provider holds some mix of:

- **Account and identity data:** name, addresses, date of birth, ID numbers used to verify you, contact info.
- **Credit and billing data:** credit checks, payment history, collections records. Rogers, for example, says in its privacy notice that it shares credit experience with credit reporting agencies and with collection agencies it retains.
- **Network usage data:** call detail records, data usage, and location and transmission metadata from your phone.
- **Customer service history:** notes on your account and recordings of calls to support.
- **Marketing data:** what you browse and which apps you use, if you are enrolled in an advertising program.

That last one surprises people. Verizon runs a "Custom Experience" program that, in its own words, "uses information about sites you visit and apps you use on your mobile device" to personalize marketing, and **"you are included in the program unless you opt out."** Verizon says it only uses the domain part of the web address, not the full URL, but it is still a record of where you go.

There are also limits on what they hold. Rogers says plainly that it does not collect the content of your phone conversations (other than calls to its own customer care, which are recorded), the content of your texts, or your internet search history. So you are mostly dealing with records *about* your activity, not the activity itself. That is still a lot.

## Step 1: Ask for a copy first

Before you ask for deletion, ask for access. There are two good reasons:

1. You find out what they actually have, so your deletion request can be specific.
2. It starts a legal clock, and how a company handles an access request tells you how seriously it takes privacy.

### In Canada: PIPEDA gives you 30 days

Private companies in Canada, including telecoms, fall under the federal privacy law, PIPEDA. Section 8 of the Act sets out how they have to handle your request:

- **Response time.** The company "shall respond to a request with due diligence and in any case not later than thirty days after receipt of the request" (s. 8(3)).
- **Extensions are limited.** They can extend by a maximum of another thirty days, and only if meeting the deadline would unreasonably interfere with their operations or consultations make it impracticable (s. 8(4)). They have to tell you about the extension.
- **No surprise fees.** They can only charge you if they told you the approximate cost first and you confirmed you are not withdrawing the request (s. 8(6)).
- **Refusals must be explained.** If they refuse, they must tell you in writing, "setting out the reasons and any recourse that they may have" (s. 8(7)).

Rogers notes in its privacy notice that "if you wish to access your personal information, you must make a request in writing." Put everything in writing anyway, for every provider. Email is fine. It gives you a date stamp.

### In the US: it depends on where you live and what service

The US has no single federal privacy law, so your rights come from a patchwork:

- **Cable subscribers.** Under the Cable Communications Policy Act, 47 U.S.C. § 551(d), "a cable subscriber shall be provided access to all personally identifiable information regarding that subscriber which is collected and maintained by a cable operator," along with a "reasonable opportunity to correct any error."
- **Phone customers.** 47 U.S.C. § 222 protects "customer proprietary network information" (CPNI), which covers the quantity, configuration, type, destination, location and amount of your telecom use. Carriers must disclose your CPNI "upon affirmative written request by the customer" to anyone you designate.
- **State privacy laws.** If you live in California or another state with a comprehensive privacy law, you have broader rights to know, access, delete and correct. Verizon's policy spells this out for California residents, including "the right to know what personal information we collect, how we use it, and how long we keep it."

## Step 2: Ask for deletion, and know the exceptions

This is where people get frustrated, so let's be honest up front: **your ISP will not delete everything, and some of the time it is legally not allowed to.** Your job is to make them delete everything they are not required to keep, and to explain the rest.

### Canada: "destroyed, erased, or made anonymous"

PIPEDA's Schedule 1 contains the ten fair information principles every company has to follow. Two of them do the heavy lifting here:

- **Principle 5, Limiting Retention.** Clause 4.5.3: "Personal information that is no longer required to fulfil the identified purposes should be destroyed, erased, or made anonymous. Organizations shall develop guidelines and implement procedures to govern the destruction of personal information."
- **Principle 9, Individual Access.** Clause 4.9.5: when you show information is inaccurate or incomplete, "the organization shall amend the information as required," and amendment can involve "correction, deletion, or addition." Where appropriate, the fix has to be passed on to third parties who have the information.

Canadian law does not give you a blanket "right to be forgotten" the way Europe does. What it gives you is the right to make the company justify why it still needs each piece of data. If you cancelled your service years ago and the bill is paid, a lot of that justification disappears.

The ISPs mostly say the same thing in their own policies. Rogers: "Rogers will only retain your personal information for as long as it is necessary to fulfill the purpose for which it was collected." It also notes that "in some instances, we have legal and regulatory obligations to retain your information and it cannot be deleted within those timeframes," and that information no longer required "will be destroyed or deidentified." TekSavvy's policy commits to keeping personal information "only as long as it remains necessary or relevant for the identified purposes or as required by law."

Hold them to their own words.

### US: cable companies must destroy old data

The strongest federal deletion rule in the US is buried in the Cable Act. 47 U.S.C. § 551(e):

> "A cable operator shall destroy personally identifiable information if the information is no longer necessary for the purpose for which it was collected and there are no pending requests or orders for access to such information."

And it has teeth. Section 551(f) lets "any person aggrieved by any act of a cable operator in violation of this section" bring a civil action in federal court, with actual or liquidated damages and attorney's fees available. If you are a former cable customer, that is worth mentioning in your letter.

For California residents, Verizon's policy confirms you can "request that we delete personal information we have about you unless we need it for specific reasons." Those reasons include providing services you asked for, maintaining an ongoing business relationship or contract, information security, exercising legal rights and complying with other laws. Translation: if you are still a customer, expect them to keep account basics. If you are a former customer, push harder.

### The exceptions you will hear

Expect pushback citing:

- **Tax and accounting rules.** Billing records usually have to be kept for a set number of years.
- **Outstanding balances or disputes.** If you owe money or there is an open complaint, they will keep the records.
- **Legal holds and court orders.** If the data is subject to a lawful request, it stays.
- **Fraud prevention.** Some identity data is kept to stop people from re-signing after fraud.

That is fine. What is not fine is "we keep everything forever because we might need it." Ask them to tell you, category by category, what they are keeping, under what obligation, and when it will be destroyed.

## Step 3: Send the request

Use the provider's privacy office, not general customer service. Front-line support reps usually cannot process privacy requests and will try to fix your bill instead.

### A letter you can copy

Adjust this for your situation. Keep it short and factual.

```
Subject: Personal information access and deletion request

To the Privacy Office,

I am a [current / former] customer. Account number (if known): [ ].
Service address(es): [ ]. Approximate dates of service: [ ].

Under [PIPEDA, s. 8 and Schedule 1 / applicable law], I request:

1. A copy of all personal information you hold about me, including
   account, billing, credit, usage, location, customer service notes
   and call recordings, and marketing or advertising profiles.
2. The list of third parties you have disclosed my information to.
3. Deletion or anonymization of all personal information that is no
   longer required for the purpose it was collected for.
4. For anything you keep, the category of information, the legal or
   business reason, and the date it will be destroyed.
5. Confirmation that I am opted out of all marketing and data-sharing
   programs.

Please respond within the 30-day statutory time limit.

[Name]
[Contact email]
```

Expect them to verify your identity. That is normal and actually a good sign: you do not want an ISP handing your records to anyone who emails them. Give them what they need to identify you and nothing more. Do not send a photo of your passport if your account number and address will do.

## Step 4: Opt out of everything while you wait

Deletion takes weeks. Opt-outs take minutes. While your request is pending, shut off the ongoing collection:

- **Advertising programs.** Look for anything like Verizon's Custom Experience in your account privacy settings and turn it off. Remember these are often on by default.
- **Telemarketing and mail.** Verizon lists 1-800-922-0204 to opt out of marketing calls and postal mail (it says removal from existing campaigns can take up to 10 days).
- **Pre-screened credit offers (US).** You can opt out at optoutprescreen.com or by calling 1-888-567-8688. Verizon's own policy points customers there.

## Step 5: If they ignore you or say no

Give them the full 30 days (plus any extension they formally told you about). Then escalate.

### Canada

1. **Reply in writing** pointing out the missed deadline or the weak refusal. Quote s. 8(3) or 8(7). Companies must have complaint procedures that are "easily accessible and simple to use" and must investigate complaints (Schedule 1, Principle 10).
2. **File with the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada.** The OPC is clear that you should "always try to resolve the issue with the organization you had a problem with before reporting a concern," and that if you skip that step "we may not be able to accept your complaint." That is why your written request matters: it is your evidence. Be realistic too. The OPC says itself that it "can help to resolve privacy complaints, but it cannot force an organization to do certain things."
3. **Quebec residents** have extra rights under Law 25, including stronger deletion and de-indexing rights, enforced by the Commission d'accès à l'information.

### US

1. **Use your state privacy law's appeal process** if you are in a state that has one.
2. **Cable customers** can point to 47 U.S.C. § 551(f) and the right to sue.
3. **File complaints** with your state attorney general and the FCC for telecom issues.

## The bigger picture: retention might get longer, not shorter

Here is the part nobody talks about. In Canada, the federal government has been pushing in the opposite direction. Bill C-22, the "lawful access" bill introduced in 2026, includes a provision letting the government make regulations requiring "core" telecommunications providers to retain certain metadata, including transmission and location data, "for reasonable periods of time not exceeding one year." Privacy experts called it risky and "unprecedented," and some VPN operators said they would leave Canada rather than comply. At committee hearings, at least one police chief said one year was "a good start" and that two or three years "would be ideal."

As of this writing, the bill has cleared the House of Commons and passed second reading in the Senate on October 22, 2026, where it was sent to committee ([LEGISinfo, Bill C-22](https://www.parl.ca/legisinfo/en/bill/45-1/c-22)). It is not law yet, and the details of what must be kept, and by whom, would come later in regulations. But the direction matters. If providers end up legally required to keep a year of your location metadata, the only data you will ever get deleted is the data outside that window. Which is one more reason to make your request now, while the default rule is still "keep it only as long as necessary."

## Quick checklist

- [ ] List every ISP and phone carrier you have had, including old ones from previous addresses.
- [ ] Send a written access request to each privacy office.
- [ ] Opt out of advertising programs, telemarketing and pre-screened credit offers.
- [ ] Review what comes back and send a specific deletion request.
- [ ] Ask what they are keeping, why, and until when.
- [ ] Calendar the 30-day deadline.
- [ ] Escalate to the Privacy Commissioner, your state AG or the FCC if they stall.

It is boring work. It is also one of the few privacy fixes where the law is actually on your side, and where a single well-written email can clear out years of records about where you lived, what you paid and where your phone went. That is worth an afternoon.
