Most people find out how much of themselves is online the same way. Someone searches their name before a job interview, or a first date, or a lease signing, and finds an old dating profile photo, a home address next to a Street View picture of the front door, or a ten-year-old classifieds ad with their phone number in it.

The instinct is to hit delete and move on. The trouble is that "delete" means something different at every company, and some of the most visible copies of you are not held by the company you signed up with at all.

I spent the last while digging through privacy policies, help pages, laws and regulator decisions to work out what really happens when you ask Google, the big social networks, dating apps, marketplaces and web archives to remove your data. This is the reader version: what to do, in what order, and what to expect.

## The short version

- **Search engines hide results. They do not delete anything.** The page stays online.
- **Platforms will delete, but almost always need you to log in or confirm by email.** Nobody else can fully finish it for you.
- **Intimate images are the one area where the law has real teeth.** In the US, platforms have 48 hours.
- **Copies are the hard part.** Archives, leaks and screenshots outlive the original.

So the order that works is: delete at the source first, then clean up search, then go after archives and copies.

## Step 1: Delete at the source

Search removal is a bandage. If the page or profile still exists, it can come back in another search engine, a new link, or a screenshot. Start with the places that actually hold the data.

### Facebook, Instagram, Threads and other social platforms

Every major social platform has a deletion path, and nearly all of them run it through your logged-in account. Meta says that "in most cases" privacy requests are handled through your password-protected account, and it may ask for government ID if you file through its webform instead ([Meta US privacy notice](https://www.facebook.com/privacy/policies/uso/)).

What to do:

1. **Download your data first** if you want a copy. Once the account is gone, so is the download tool.
2. **Delete the account from the account settings**, not just deactivate it. Deactivation keeps everything.
3. **Watch your email.** Most platforms send a confirmation link, and the request stalls until you click it.
4. **Note the date.** Deletion is not instant. Meta's help pages say copies "may remain" in backups after its 90-day deletion window ([Facebook Help](https://www.facebook.com/help/224562897555674)).

If you can no longer log in to an old account, use the platform's privacy request form and name the email address or phone number the account used. The company will usually verify you by writing to that address, so it helps to recover access to old email accounts before you start.

Twitter, TikTok and LinkedIn all describe similar request routes in their privacy policies, and each asks for some kind of proof that the request is really yours.

### Dating apps: the profile you forgot about

There is no dating company that will tell you whether an account exists for an email address. That is a good privacy decision, but it means you have to ask.

The workable check is a **privacy or data access request naming your own email address**. The company confirms by writing to that address and then tells you what it holds. Grindr describes exactly this process in its privacy policy ([Grindr](https://trust.grindr.com/en-US/privacy)). Match Group, which owns Tinder, Hinge, OkCupid, Match and Plenty of Fish, says it provides data copies "regardless of where they live", and it keeps a record of past accounts for three years, so an account you closed a while ago may still be confirmable ([Match Group privacy](https://mtch.com/trust-safety/privacy/)).

What to do:

1. **List every app you can remember using**, including the ones you deleted from your phone years ago. Deleting the app does not delete the account.
2. **Send a data access request from each email you might have used.** Ask what they hold and whether a closed account still exists.
3. **Then ask for deletion** of the account and the data, in writing.
4. **Do not trust the download as complete.** On 13 November 2026, Ireland's Data Protection Commission fined Tinder's EU operator €11 million because its data download tool left out categories of data without telling users ([DPC](https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/news-media/latest-news/data-protection-commission-fines-mtch-eu11-million-following-inquiry-tinder-users-personal-data)). Match Group says it will appeal. If something looks missing, ask specifically.

Photos can travel further than you think. In 2026 the FTC took action against OkCupid's operators over sharing user photos with a facial recognition company, and that company certified it had deleted about 3 million OkCupid photos and the models trained on them ([FTC case page](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/okcupidmatch)). That is a rare case of a downstream copy actually being destroyed, and it took a federal regulator to get there.

### Marketplaces and classifieds

Old ads are a quiet source of phone numbers and neighbourhoods.

- **Kijiji** takes deletion requests by email to its support team or by mail to its privacy officer in Toronto, according to its privacy policy updated 2 June 2026. Its deletion page says requests go through the app with your password, but also tells people locked out of their account to contact support ([Kijiji privacy policy](https://community.kijiji.ca/t/privacy-policy-for-kijiji/37)).
- **Craigslist** handles privacy requests at ccpa@craigslist.org ([craigslist privacy policy](https://www.craigslist.org/about/privacy.policy)).
- **Facebook Marketplace** listings go with your Facebook account, so the step above covers them.

## Step 2: Clean up Google and Bing

Once the source is gone, search results usually drop out on their own over time. For anything still there, or anything you could not get removed at the source, ask the search engine.

### Google

Google has several routes, and picking the right one matters:

- **Results about you** lets you request removal of results showing your phone number, home address, email and similar contact details. You need a Google account, and it can alert you when new results appear.
- **The personal information removal form** covers things like doxxing, ID numbers, and non-consensual explicit images. Google says it now removes duplicates of approved intimate images as standard ([Google Search Help](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/16305143)).
- **Images of minors** can be removed at the request of someone under 18, or by a parent or relative.

Two things to know before you file. First, approval may be **partial**: Google can hide a URL only for searches that include your name, so the page still shows up for other searches. Second, a removal from Google does not touch the page itself. Google says the content "may still be reachable through links, social media, or other search engines" ([Google Search Help](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/9673730)).

### Street View: blur your house

If your home is the problem, Google Maps lets you request a permanent blur of your house, car or face in Street View. Google may ask for proof that you live there, such as a lease or a utility bill, and says it deletes those documents after 60 days. The blur only covers Google's own imagery, not copies or AI images made by others from it ([Google Maps Help](https://support.google.com/maps/answer/15439776?hl=en)).

Request the blur yourself, from your own account. It is quick, and it is one of the most effective single steps for people worried about someone finding their front door.

### Bing (and DuckDuckGo)

Bing powers a lot of DuckDuckGo's results, so a Bing removal helps twice. Be realistic, though: Microsoft says reporting "will not necessarily result in removal" and that it limits removals to a narrow set of circumstances. For North Americans that mostly means intimate images and similar serious harms. File anyway if the content qualifies, but do not expect Google-style contact info removal.

### If you are in Canada

Canada does not yet have a clear legal "right to be forgotten" in search. In 2025 the Privacy Commissioner found that Google's search engine falls under federal privacy law and recommended that Google de-list articles from searches for a complainant's name. **Google refused**, saying the courts should decide ([OPC finding](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2025/pipeda-2025-002/)). In late August 2026 the Commissioner went to the Federal Court to enforce the recommendation ([OPC news release](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-news/news-and-announcements/2026/nr-c_260828/)). Until that is decided, Canadians outside Quebec are relying on Google's own policies.

**Quebec residents have more.** Under the province's private-sector privacy law as amended by Law 25, you can ask a business to stop spreading your information or to de-index a link attached to your name when the harm to you is serious and clearly outweighs the public interest and free expression. You can also demand deletion of data collected unlawfully. Requests are made in writing, the business must respond within 30 days (some guides read this as 30 working days), silence counts as a refusal, and you can get a free confirmation that your data was deleted ([amended Act, unofficial consolidation](https://www.lavery.ca/upload/pdf/Publications/Amended-Private-Sector-Act-P-39-1.pdf)). If you live in Quebec, say so in your request and cite the Act.

## Step 3: Intimate images, the one place the law moves fast

If someone has posted an intimate image of you without consent, including an AI-generated fake, stop reading general advice and use the dedicated routes.

**In the US, the TAKE IT DOWN Act requires covered platforms to remove the image within 48 hours** of a valid request, and to make reasonable efforts to remove known copies ([Public Law 119-12](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ12/html/PLAW-119publ12.htm)). The FTC has enforced it since 19 May 2026. A valid notice needs four things:

1. Your signature (or that of someone authorized to act for you).
2. Enough information to find the image, such as the URL.
3. A good-faith statement that it was shared without your consent.
4. Your contact details.

The FTC's guidance says the protection is "not limited to individuals who hold an account on your platform", so you do not need to be a user of the site ([FTC guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-take-it-down-act)).

Also use the hashing services. **StopNCII.org** (for adults) and **NCMEC's Take It Down** (for images taken when you were under 18) create a digital fingerprint of the image on your own device, so the picture itself never leaves your phone, and participating platforms use that fingerprint to block uploads.

Meta's intimate image reporting form works even if you do not have an account. Google and Bing both have dedicated forms for search results.

**In British Columbia**, the Intimate Images Protection Act lets the Civil Resolution Tribunal order websites and other internet intermediaries to delete and de-index an image, with daily penalties for ignoring the order. Elsewhere in Canada, sharing intimate images without consent is a criminal offence, and reporting to police is an option alongside the platform routes.

## Step 4: Archives and copies

This is the part people skip, and it matters more than it used to. Google retired its cached page links in 2024 and began linking to the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine instead, which makes archived copies of old pages easier to find than before.

- **The Internet Archive** takes removal requests at info@archive.org. Include the exact URLs, the time period you want excluded, and why. It makes no promises about the outcome, and the process is built around people who controlled the site or account, so results are less certain if you are just named on someone else's page ([Internet Archive Help](https://help.archive.org/help/how-do-i-request-to-remove-something-from-archive-org/)). It has a separate form for intimate images.
- **archive.today** has no published privacy removal process that I could find. That is worth knowing before you spend hours looking for one.
- **Data leaks cannot be recalled.** The 2015 Ashley Madison breach of roughly 36 million accounts was still being used in extortion emails five years later. If your data was in a breach, the useful moves are changing passwords, turning on two-factor authentication, and treating any "we have your data" email as a scam until proven otherwise.

## A note on removal services

Paid data removal services are genuinely useful for data brokers and people-search sites, where they can file hundreds of opt-outs you would never get around to. Just know where they stop. Optery's custom removals cover only brokers and people-search sites and exclude LinkedIn, Facebook and Twitter, and it reports about a 75% success rate that can take up to eight months ([Optery help](https://help.optery.com/en/article/ultimate-plan-offers-best-effort-removals-for-unsupported-data-brokers-through-custom-requests-lchi5e/)). OneRep does not remove content from blogs, news, government or review sites.

That matches what I found across the board: a service can file the request for you, but the platforms will almost always send the last step, a login or a confirmation email, back to you. If a service promises it can fully delete your Facebook or dating profile without you lifting a finger, ask how.

## Your checklist

1. Make a list: social accounts, dating apps, marketplaces, old email addresses.
2. Download anything you want to keep.
3. Delete accounts from inside each app or site, and click every confirmation email.
4. For accounts you cannot access, send a written privacy request naming the email or phone number used.
5. Request a Street View blur of your home.
6. Use Google's Results about you and removal forms for anything still showing.
7. File with Bing if the content is serious enough to qualify.
8. If you are in Quebec, cite Law 25 in your requests.
9. For intimate images, use TAKE IT DOWN notices, StopNCII.org or Take It Down, and the platform forms right away.
10. Email the Internet Archive about archived copies.
11. Keep a simple log: where you filed, the date, and what they said. Check back in a month.

None of this makes you invisible, and anyone who says otherwise is selling something. But working through it in this order removes the easiest paths to you: the search result, the forgotten profile, the photo of your front door. For most people, that is what matters.
